Policy brief, October 2026

Keeping Southern California's sand moving

The region needs millions of cubic yards of sand to hold its beaches, rail line and coastal roads in place, but it depends on dredges based thousands of miles away. This brief proposes a partner-financed Pacific sand fleet operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, a single coalition to permit and fund the work once, and a short-term plan to put sand on the beach by rail while the fleet is built. Both come with cost models anyone can adjust.

The problem

Dams, flood channels and development have cut off most of the sand that rivers once carried to Southern California's beaches. The San Diego region's 1993 Shoreline Preservation Strategy estimated about 30 million cubic yards were needed to build protective beaches. The two regional projects since then, in 2001 and 2012, placed about 3.6 million.

SANDAG's 2025 feasibility study for a third Regional Beach Sand Project (RBSP III) proposes about 6.35 million cy across 15 San Diego County beaches and, for the first time, three in south Orange County: Dana Point, San Clemente North Beach and San Clemente State Beach. It estimates $278 million for the combined program and a benefit-cost ratio of 5.5, counting recreation only.

South Orange County sand is the most expensive in the program, about $39–43 per cubic yard, because the only confirmed borrow site is off Surfside/Sunset, far up the coast. Trucking from inland sources cannot fill the gap. It works for small opportunistic loads, but at program scale the truck trips, road impacts and lack of compatible inland sand make it impractical.

The deeper problem is equipment. Offshore sand has to be moved by ocean-going hopper dredges, and very few are based on the West Coast. Most of the private fleet works the Gulf and East Coast, where the volume is. When a dredge does come west, the region pays a mobilization bill RBSP III puts at $15 million, competes for a narrow slot in the contractor's schedule, and risks standby charges or a departed dredge if an environmental window, permit or weather delay pushes the work back.

We need sand now: the short-term plan

A dredge fleet is years away. The beaches are losing sand this winter. In the last month San Clemente, Dana Point, Newport Beach and Laguna Beach have each declared coastal emergencies; San Clemente put $1 million toward sand for North Beach and OCTA declared its own emergency to place 690,000 cubic yards. The money and the urgency are there. What is missing is a way to move sand at volume. OCTA itself notes that the equipment needed to deliver sand is often booked months out, and the small lifts that are possible on short notice are brutally expensive: the first emergency delivery to North Beach in 2025 brought about 3,400 cubic yards from a Palm Springs quarry by truck for roughly $900,000, well over $250 per cubic yard.

No more rock on the beach. Sand is the shoreline protection structure.

No more riprap

Rock protects the track. It does not protect the beach, and it makes the beach in front of it disappear. Three things happen when riprap goes on a sandy shore. It buries the beach it sits on. Waves that would have run up a sloping beach and lost their energy instead hit the rock and reflect, scouring sand from the toe of the revetment and from the unprotected beach at each end. And as the shoreline keeps retreating against a structure that cannot move, the beach in front of it drowns, a process coastal engineers call passive erosion. Every emergency revetment placed along the San Clemente corridor since 2021 has traded a narrow strip of beach for a protected track. OCTA has recognized this: its current emergency program is about 95% sand and 5% rock, with rock only where a slope has to be stabilized. The coalition's position should be simple. Rock only where the track will otherwise fail, sand everywhere else, and a standing commitment to replace rock with sand retention over time.

What OCTA should build this winter instead of a wall

On September 28, 2026 OCTA’s board declared a seasonal emergency ahead of El Niño that authorizes three things at the south end of town: repair of existing riprap, an “engineered shoreline protection structure” along about three-quarters of a mile south of San Clemente State Beach, and 690,000 cubic yards of sand, 240,000 between North Beach and Mariposa Point and 450,000 south of the State Beach. The structure has no final design yet. OCTA’s earlier application to the Coastal Commission described a seawall or revetment of roughly 1,200 feet across San Clemente State Beach and Calafia, and the agency has separately applied to make the 26,000 tons of emergency rock placed at Cyprus Shores in 2021-22 permanent. The beach in front of that rock is gone, which is the clearest local evidence of what a wall does.

The sand is the right answer and it is already in the declaration. The question is sequence. Rock can be placed in weeks and sand, by OCTA’s own account, waits on equipment that is booked months out, so the emergency pushes the agency toward building the structure first and placing sand later. The coalition’s position should be the reverse, and it should come with the means to do it.

  • Make the sand the structure. An engineered beach, a wide berm with a sand dune at the embankment toe, built to a designed crest height and width, is a shoreline protection structure. It absorbs wave energy rather than reflecting it, it is walkable, it feeds the beaches downcoast as it erodes, and it can be rebuilt. Put the 450,000 cubic yards for Area 4 on the beach as a designed berm and dune before the January swells, and the wall is not needed this winter.
  • Deliver it in sixty days, not six months. At the model’s defaults the 450,000 cubic yards is 225 conveyor-train loads: a year at one train a night, but about eight weeks if OCTA grants a scheduled closure window of four trains a day, with trucks from the Gillibrand quarry and, if a dock can be arranged, scows pumping ashore running alongside. The corridor absorbed a six-week closure in 2025 to place rock; a shorter one to place sand is the better trade. This costs more per yard than waiting for a dredge. It is cheaper than a wall plus the sand mitigation the Coastal Commission will require for the wall anyway, and it leaves a beach.
  • Where the embankment truly cannot wait for sand, use a buried toe, not a wall. A rock toe set at the base of the embankment and buried under the sand berm protects the track in the storm that strips the berm, stays out of sight and out of the surf zone the rest of the time, and does not reflect waves off a bare face. Geotextile sand containers do the same job and can be removed. Either should come with a removal or burial condition and a sand replacement schedule, so emergency rock does not become a permanent wall by default, as it has at Cyprus Shores.
  • Try a cobble berm where the beach is already lost. At Cotton’s Point and Cyprus Shores, where there is no sand left to protect, a dynamic cobble revetment, loose cobble graded to the size the surf can rearrange but not remove, dissipates wave energy the way a natural cobble beach does. Ventura’s Surfers’ Point has run on one since 2011. It is a nature-based alternative to a seawall that the Coastal Commission has approved before.
  • Keep sand on the beach once it is there. A programmatic retention project, a short groin field or a submerged reef off the State Beach, is the difference between placing 450,000 cubic yards once and placing it every three years. RBSP III proposes a pilot retention structure for San Diego County; San Clemente State Beach, where OCTA’s own numbers put the most sand, is the obvious Orange County pilot.
  • Make the wall the last resort, with a sunset. If, after the berm is built and a storm takes it, the embankment is still failing in a specific reach, a structure there is defensible. Permit it as temporary, bond its removal, and tie it to the long-term fix OCTA is already studying for the corridor.

None of this asks OCTA to accept more risk to the track. It asks the agency to spend the first dollars and the first weeks on the measure that protects both the railroad and the beach, and to treat rock as what its own program already says it is: the last five percent.

Sand by rail

The rail line that makes the beach a problem is also the solution. The track runs on the beach from Capistrano Beach through North Beach, the pier, San Clemente State Beach and Cyprus Shores. Herzog's Automated Conveyor Train is a 30-car consist, each car carrying up to 98 tons, with a discharge car that places material up to 50 feet from track center at up to 2,000 tons an hour. One loaded train carries roughly 2,000 cubic yards of sand and can be emptied onto the beach in about two hours. Material is discharged on the field side with the boom locked away from the live track, which is how the system is built to avoid fouling ballast on adjacent lines.

The nearest source is a few miles up the canyon. P.W. Gillibrand Co. operates a sand quarry at 31302 Ortega Highway in San Juan Capistrano, the same material OC Parks trucked onto Capistrano Beach this spring, and it sits close to the rail corridor where the line passes through San Juan Capistrano. Sand can be trucked a short distance to a rail loadout there and run the last eight to ten miles to the beach on the conveyor train, or trucked straight to Capistrano Beach and North Beach. The larger long-run source is inland: in wet years the Santa Ana River delivers sand that the county and Newport Beach already harvest at the river outlet, and the Prado Dam basin near Corona holds about 80 million cubic yards of accumulated sediment half a mile from the Metrolink line, where UC Irvine is studying loading railcars for exactly this purpose; that material needs screening to meet beach-compatibility standards and a loadout that does not yet exist, and it travels about 70 miles of existing track through Fullerton and Irvine with no transload.

The operating question is the time slot. There are gaps between the last Surfliner and the first morning train, and freight already moves through the corridor at night. One conveyor train per night, five nights a week, places on the order of 500,000 cubic yards a year, roughly OCTA's entire emergency program in twelve months. The corridor has absorbed six-week closures for emergency rock work; a scheduled overnight sand window is a smaller ask with a far better result for the track. With enough committed volume, Herzog has reason to station a second conveyor train on the West Coast.

Two pieces of work already under way sharpen the picture. The City of San Clemente’s Offshore Sand Source Investigation took vibracores at the nearby candidate sites in 2025 and found the closest ones unusable: Dana Point, Doheny, North Beach, San Clemente South and San Mateo Point are mostly fine silt, and a Santa Margarita River core came back about 90% fines. San Onofre was the promising site, with the best material in the top seven feet nearest shore, and the Santa Ana River and Surfside-Sunset offshore deposits tested suitable. Phase 2 exploration was planned for summer 2026 with a final report due in December, and its answer on San Onofre decides whether a dredge borrow site exists within a few miles of San Clemente or whether the long-term sand comes from the Santa Ana River and north. Separately, the Orange County Water District is working with a UC Irvine engineering team on a sediment-by-rail feasibility assessment for Prado Dam and the Santa Ana River. It counts roughly 1.5 million cubic yards of sediment available this year at five upstream sites, screens a dozen or more downstream staging and rail-spur sites with Doheny Beach as the worked example, and compares rail, trucking, conveyor and slurry delivery. Its early conclusion matches this brief: rail pays over distance, and the transload cost at each end is what decides it. The coalition should adopt both studies rather than repeat them.

Where the loadout goes

Sand reaches the train at a loadout: a siding of about 2,000 feet for a 30-car consist, five to ten acres for a stockpile, a loader, and truck access that avoids residential streets. Three places on the line qualify, and each trades something.

  • North San Juan Capistrano, along Camino Capistrano. Closest to the Gillibrand quarry, about six miles down Ortega Highway and one exit up I-5, with commercial and industrial frontage beside the track and no need to pass through the historic downtown. A new siding on OCTA right-of-way, roughly nine rail miles to North Beach. The simplest truck logistics; the most neighbors.
  • Doheny Park Road, Capistrano Beach. Industrial parcels beside the right-of-way where the line meets the coast, near the existing Serra siding, which may cut the track work. Minutes by rail from Capistrano Beach and North Beach, and the site the UC Irvine team used as its downstream example. Trucks come through Dana Point, which is the hard part.
  • San Onofre, south of Cyprus Shores: the plant site. The San Onofre nuclear plant is about 84% dismantled, with the domes due down by the end of 2027 and site work finishing in 2028. What it leaves behind is the best industrial ground on this coast: a working rail spur that has shipped nearly 2,500 railcars of material to Utah, a mile of sea wall and a beach where the plant’s steam generators were landed by barge, acres of paved laydown yard, and freeway access at Basilone Road with no homes anywhere near. It is the only existing rail loadout on the corridor south of the county line and a natural dock for the barge option. Trains loaded here place from south to north starting at San Clemente State Beach, and the sand still comes from Gillibrand or Prado, since the cores off the base came back too fine. Three things have to be worked through. The land is the Navy’s and Edison’s lease requires it to remove most facilities when it ends, so the coalition needs the Navy and Edison to agree now to keep the spur and part of the yard in place rather than tear them out, and a new lease afterward. The spent fuel stays on site indefinitely in a federally licensed storage installation with its own security plan, barriers and standoff zone, so the yard sits at the opposite end of the site with trucks entering from the freeway and never approaching the storage perimeter, loads come from known sources on fixed routes with sealed trucks and credentialed drivers, and an inspection checkpoint screens vehicles up the road; the security plan is reviewed by Edison, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and the Marine Corps before anything moves. And the ground is not free until 2028, which makes this the site for the dredge era and the barge option rather than the first year of rail sand.

A loadout at either end of the corridor also lets the train work in both directions, placing on the way out and on the way back; two loadouts, one north and one south, would make that routine. The loadout selector in the short-term model sets the truck leg, the rail miles to each beach and a siding-and-yard cost for each site.

The SONGS site: the best southern staging ground

Six miles south of Cyprus Shores, the San Onofre Nuclear Generating Station is in the final stages of decommissioning. Southern California Edison reported 84% complete in mid-2026 with a target of year-end 2028. When the demolition of the twin domes is done, roughly 84 acres of paved industrial ground becomes available on Camp Pendleton, and it has the infrastructure the coalition needs most.

  • A working rail spur. Nearly 2,500 rail car loads of low-level waste have shipped out of the site to Utah since 2020. The loading tracks and the connection to the LOSSAN main line are in place. This is the only existing rail loadout on the corridor south of the county line, and it is the most important asset on the list.
  • A barge landing precedent. The replacement steam generators were delivered to this site by barge and brought ashore across the beach in 2009-2011. A scow unloading here follows a path that has already been done.
  • Paved laydown ground and freeway access. Basilone Road off I-5, no residential streets, and far more stockpile space than a sand terminal needs.

Two constraints are real. The Navy owns the land and Edison’s lease requires removal of most facilities at the end; the rail spur and yard pavement need to be explicitly carved out of the restoration obligation before the cleanup crews take them. And 123 canisters of spent nuclear fuel remain on site indefinitely, in hardened dry-cask storage, while the federal government works out a permanent disposal site.

The security question is the right one to ask. The answer is that the site is already more secure than any commercial alternative. The casks are 5-inch stainless steel inside reinforced concrete vaults, engineered to withstand aircraft impact and severe storms; the NRC treats vehicle-borne threats as a design-basis event and requires standoff and barriers as part of the storage license. Every truck entering the site first clears a Marine Corps access-control point with ID verification, manifests and the ability to hold any vehicle. Vehicle screening at that gate, under-vehicle inspection systems and drive-through imaging portals are mature technology deployed at sensitive sites nationally, and adding them as a condition of a sand-terminal lease costs a fraction of the site’s value. A sand terminal here is subject to a formal NRC and Navy security review as part of any lease, and that review is the right place to establish standoff and inspection requirements, not the brief. The base perimeter makes this safer than a public dock in Dana Point.

The practical step is to approach Edison and the Navy now, before the demolition contractor removes the spur under the lease’s restoration clause. Keeping the rail tracks and a few acres of pavement costs Edison nothing and saves the coalition the single largest capital item in the southern plan. This is a 2028-2029 site for a stockpile and rail loadout, which aligns with the dredge timeline rather than the first year of rail sand, and makes the short-term investment in a northern loadout worthwhile because both ends of the corridor will eventually be served.

Will sand foul the ballast? Why the conveyor train is the safe way to do this

The concern is legitimate and specific. Ballast, the crushed rock the track sits in, works because it drains and because it holds the ties firmly; fines that migrate into it reduce both, and on a signaled line damp, salty material in the ballast can also lower the electrical resistance that the track circuits depend on. That is why no one should be dumping sand on or beside the rails. It is also why the Automated Conveyor Train is the right tool rather than a risk.

The train never puts sand on the track structure. Its discharge car carries a 35-foot boom that places material up to 50 feet from track center, well beyond the ballast shoulder and the toe of the embankment, at an angle of up to 45 degrees to either side, and the boom locks on the field side so it cannot swing back over the live track. Sand goes onto the seaward beach, below track grade, where it belongs. The same machine is used by Class I railroads to deliver ballast itself and to rebuild washed-out track, work that depends on exactly this control. A few operating rules close the remaining gap: discharge only with the boom at its outer reach, place in a windrow that dozers spread the same night, mist the windrow against windblown dust, hold the window when winds are onshore and strong, use beach-compatible sand with the low fines content the permits already require, and run a track inspection and a sweep before the first train in the morning. Herzog has told the coalition the train can do this without fouling the ballast; the operating rules make that a condition of the contract rather than a promise.

Two points put the risk in proportion. First, OCTA’s own plan places 540,000 cubic yards of sand right beside this track, by truck and by dredge; the sand ends up in the same place whichever vehicle brings it, so the question is which delivery method controls placement best, and a boom that drops sand at a fixed distance from the rail controls it better than a dump truck backing down a beach ramp. Second, the track already lives on a beach. Windblown sand and salt spray reach the ballast today, and the storms that overtop the line leave cobble, kelp and wet sand on the ties. A wider beach is the thing that stops that. The way to settle it is a pilot: one train, one night, with track geometry and ballast resistance measured before and after, witnessed by Metrolink’s track engineers. If the numbers move, the rules change; if they do not, the objection is answered.

Trucks still matter, and there is a smarter way to run them. Trucking straight from a quarry to the beach is limited by how many trips a beach access point can take in a day and by the season when the beach is open to work. A staging yard near the corridor changes that: long-haul transfer trucks, which carry about 24 cubic yards against 10 for a ten-wheeler and cost less per mile, run to the yard year-round; a loader and a conveyor or short shuttle move sand to the beach when the window is open. Rather than lease a fleet and hire drivers, the coalition should sign a four- to five-year haul contract with a guaranteed annual volume. The commitment is what cuts the price: a hauler who knows the work is there for five years can lock in trucks and drivers, price below the spot rate, and fill the months the beach is closed with commercial work a public fleet legally cannot take, so the idle-truck risk sits with the hauler rather than the coalition. The model shows where that pays: on a long haul like Prado it cuts the trucking cost by roughly a fifth to a quarter, while on the short run from Gillibrand the double handling cancels most of the gain. An owned fleet of leased trucks saves a little more on paper, but only if other public agencies keep the trucks busy in the off months, and it leaves the coalition paying for parked trucks the moment a permit slips; the guaranteed contract captures most of the saving without that exposure.

A third path avoids the hopper dredge altogether. Inland sand trucked to a dock can be loaded onto scows, towed a few miles and pumped onto the beach through a pipeline by a pump-out unit of the small hydraulic-dredge class, the equipment the 2009 Orange County study found a county could own economically. It costs about what rail costs per yard because the sand is handled four times, but it needs no $250 million vessel and no offshore borrow site, it reaches beaches the track does not, including Laguna, and the pump barge is a sensible first vessel for a coalition that wants to own equipment. Its limit is the dock: Dana Point Harbor is close but shallow and residential, Camp Pendleton’s Del Mar boat basin is a working harbor 15 miles south, and Long Beach has everything but a 50-mile tow. What the earlier studies found

California Department of Boating and Waterways, 2008. Moffatt & Nichol advised the state not to buy a dredge. Contracted beach dredging then cost about $6 per cy, compared with about $8.66 for a state-owned hopper dredge, and the study expected the private industry to station dredges in California once steady work appeared.

Orange County Parks, 2009. The same firm reached the opposite answer for OC's small harbor maintenance work. A county-owned cutter-suction dredge with ocean disposal barges would cost about 10% less than contracting, because the county could spread the equipment cost over a dependable workload. It also noted that a pooled, multi-year contract could capture similar savings.

SANDAG RBSP III, 2025. The newest study lists dredge ownership or a long-term lease as an open decision and recommends a separate cost-benefit analysis of leasing.

The 2008 conclusion has not aged well. Contracted beach sand now costs several times what that study assumed, and the industry has not stationed dredges in the region. The 2009 study's core insight still holds: ownership wins when there is enough reliable volume to keep the dredge working.

The proposal: a partner-financed Pacific sand fleet

No single city has enough work to justify a hopper dredge. San Clemente's share of RBSP III is roughly 200,000 cy a year, while one modern hopper dredge can place several million cy in a season. The volume exists only at the regional scale.

Rather than create a new local agency to own and insure a ship, the region would fund the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to build or acquire and operate one or more hopper dredges stationed in Southern California, with costs shared among:

  • The federal government, through Corps construction and shore protection funding and a dredge built under the Corps' fleet program.
  • The State of California, through beach restoration and coastal resilience funding.
  • OCTA, because erosion along the LOSSAN rail corridor through San Clemente repeatedly shuts down passenger and freight service.
  • Coastal cities and counties, in proportion to the sand they receive.

The dredge would place beach sand during environmental windows and perform paid navigation and harbor maintenance when those windows close, keeping it busy year-round. That off-season work is what spreads the fixed cost and brings the price per cubic yard down.

The national-interest case is stronger than beaches alone. The fleet would protect the LOSSAN corridor, the second-busiest intercity rail route in the country; the shoreline at Marine Corps Base Camp Pendleton; Navy installations in San Diego; Pacific Coast Highway; and the region's ports. It would also give the Corps a second West Coast hopper dredge for emergencies at a time when its two Portland-based vessels are more than 40 years old.

What has to change

Congress has to authorize it. Since 1978, federal law has steered hopper dredging to private industry. It limits how much work Corps hopper dredges can take on and caps operating days for the West Coast dredges Essayons and Yaquina. A Southern California fleet would need new authority, most likely in a Water Resources Development Act. The private dredging industry can be expected to oppose it.

The capital cost is large. The Corps' new 6,000 cy hopper dredge, the Donnelly, is being built under a $257 million fixed-price contract and took about four years to build. A smaller vessel designed for beach work, or a refurbished one, could cost less.

The price is the market, not mainly the bureaucracy. It is fair to ask whether a federal procurement inflated the $257 million. The private comparables say mostly no. Great Lakes Dredge & Dock's 6,330 cy Amelia Island, built at Conrad Shipyard and delivered in 2025, represented a total investment of about $246 million. Weeks Marine's 8,550 cy Magdalen cost about $110 million in 2017 and the 15,000 cy Frederick Paup about $200 million at its 2020 contract; a hopper dredge today simply costs two to three times what it did a decade ago. The drivers are the Jones Act and the 1906 Dredging Act, which require any vessel dredging in US waters to be built in a US yard; Tier IV engines; and a handful of yards building one or two dredges at a time. Two things do follow from the comparables. Bigger hulls cost far less per cubic yard of hopper, so a larger dredge shared across the region is cheaper per load than a small one. And the agencies that build them cheaply, Great Lakes, Weeks and Manson, do it with an in-house owner's team, a proven foreign design licensed from Royal IHC or C-Job, and sister-ship options that amortize engineering across two hulls.

Build the team, not the shipyard. A coalition yard in Long Beach would cost more than the dredges and would compete for the same scarce shipwrights that make California yards expensive. A cheaper coast already exists: the Gulf yards in Louisiana, Texas and Florida build every US hopper dredge today, and a foreign yard is off the table by law. What the coalition should fund is a small commissioning team, an owner's representative, naval architect and marine engineer, that picks a proven design, negotiates a two-hull option, holds the yard to schedule, and runs the Corps' or the JPA's procurement in parallel with authorization rather than after it. That team is also what makes a used-hull purchase or a long-term charter possible on a short clock. The presets in the dredge model let you compare a Corps build, a private-style build, a larger hull and a refurbished vessel.

Crews are civilian. Corps dredges are crewed by federal merchant mariners, not military personnel, and are paid at maritime prevailing wages. The savings in this proposal come mainly from federal financing, self-insurance and year-round use, not from lower labor costs.

Four years is possible only one way. The Corps' new dredge took four years from contract award to delivery, after more than a year of design and procurement, and a West Coast fleet still needs congressional authorization first. A new-build fleet in four years is not realistic. Two dedicated dredges in four years is realistic only if the coalition secures authorization in the next Water Resources Development Act, funds the acquisition of an existing US-built hopper dredge or a long-term charter rather than a new hull, and starts the Corps' procurement in parallel. The working timeline this brief assumes: sand by rail within 12 months, a multi-year charter or contract dredge within 2 to 3 years, the first owned dredge within 4 to 5 years, and a second within 6 to 8.

Closer sand sources matter in every scenario. Haul distance drives the cost of south Orange County sand. Investigating borrow sites nearer Dana Point and San Clemente, such as San Mateo Point, Camp Pendleton and Newport Beach, lowers costs whoever runs the dredge.

Utilization is the make-or-break assumption. If the dredge cannot find enough off-season work, the case weakens considerably. The model below lets you test that directly.

Newport to San Clemente: one coast, different needs

The dredge fleet section follows SANDAG's RBSP III footprint, which ends at Dana Point. The coalition should not. Every beach city in Orange County south of the Santa Ana River is now dealing with the same winter, and each brings something different to the table.

  • Newport Beach already runs a sand program: it takes sand from the Santa Ana River outlet most years, and under this fall's emergency agreement with the county it can move up to 200,000 cubic yards onto its shoreline by January 2027 for about $1 million to $1.5 million. Its long-term need is less about beach sand and more about keeping Newport Harbor and the river outlet dredged, which is exactly the off-season work that keeps a regional dredge busy and cheap.
  • Laguna Beach has sheltered pocket beaches that have held up better, has declared an emergency, and says it does not plan a sand project of its own yet. It cannot be served by rail. Its role is funding, political weight and a modest truck-placed allocation when it wants one.
  • Dana Point has lost most of Capistrano Beach, has red-tagged oceanfront homes, and spent $440,000 this spring to truck 13,500 cubic yards onto the beach. RBSP III proposes 500,000 cubic yards at Dana Point, and the rail line runs along Capistrano Beach, so it can take sand by conveyor train.
  • San Clemente is the center of the problem: a sand deficit the city puts at two to four million cubic yards, the rail corridor that keeps closing, and the only place where the fastest delivery method, rail, and the largest long-term need coincide.

North of the Santa Ana River the picture changes. Huntington and Seal Beach sit in the San Pedro littoral cell and are fed by the Corps' Surfside-Sunset project, so sand placed there does not reach the south county. They belong in the coalition as partners and as a borrow source, not as receiver beaches in this plan.

One roof, one permit, one war chest

The cost model below prices dredging and placement only. The other costs are real and they are mostly paid again every time a project starts from scratch. RBSP III budgets about $8.2 million just to get to a permitted design: environmental review, permitting, habitat mapping, biological reports, offshore borrow-site investigation, receiver-site sampling, surveys and final engineering. Construction adds about $5.4 million in compliance reporting, biological and shoreline monitoring, and post-construction surveys, plus a 35% contingency that reflects how uncertain cost and schedule are at this stage. On top of that sit agency staff time, Coastal Act mitigation obligations, air permits for the dredge, and the risk of litigation. The study itself expects construction within five to ten years, and warns that folding the work into an existing Corps project could take decades.

Much of that time and money comes from fragmentation: SANDAG, the Corps, OCTA, the state, OC Parks and each city run separate projects, with separate environmental documents, permits, consultants and funding cycles, often placing sand on the same stretch of beach. Consolidating under one coastal sediment coalition changes three things.

  • One environmental document and one set of permits for a 50-year programmatic nourishment regime, which RBSP III already recommends, so that each five-year cycle is a notice, not a new project.
  • One pooled fund. Real money is already on the table. In fall 2024 OCTA secured $305 million for coastal rail stabilization through San Clemente: $100 million from the Federal Railroad Administration’s CRISI program, $125 million from the state’s Transit and Intercity Rail Capital Program, and $80 million from the California Transportation Commission’s trade corridor program, with OCTA’s Measure M covering the rest of a program estimated at $250 million to $340 million. The Corps’ San Clemente shore protection project, Newport’s annual river-outlet program, OC Parks’ Capistrano Beach placements and the cities’ emergency appropriations sit alongside it, each with its own rules. Pooling them, with a shared agreement on how much of the rail money goes to sand rather than walls, is the war chest. The funding block in the model totals what is secured and shows the gap the coalition still has to close.
  • Emergency and expedited authority used deliberately. The tools exist. In 2025 OCTA applied to the California Coastal Commission for an emergency coastal development permit on March 31 to protect the rail line through San Clemente; a partial permit was issued April 10, and the agency planned to place up to 540,000 cubic yards of sand and repair riprap within 18 months using about $305 million in secured funding. The work stayed subject to Coastal Act mitigation and monitoring, which is exactly the model: the environmental protections remain, but the queue disappears. Federal emergency authorities for infrastructure protection and a declared state of emergency along the coastal rail corridor could give a coalition the same footing.

Two cautions. Emergency permits are meant for urgent, limited work and the Coastal Commission scrutinizes them closely, so the durable path is a programmatic permit obtained once, with emergency authority reserved for the first placement and genuine failures. And speed that looks like skipping review invites lawsuits that can cost more time than the review itself. The goal is to go from five to ten years to roughly 18 to 24 months for the first sand, then never re-permit again.

Next steps

  1. Get a quote from Herzog for a dedicated conveyor train and ask OCTA, Metrolink and BNSF for a standing overnight sand window through San Clemente.
  2. Line up sand: a sediment agreement for the Santa Ana River outlet and Prado basin with the county, OCWD and the Corps, and a supply contract with the San Juan Capistrano quarry for truck-served beaches.
  3. Ask the Navy and Southern California Edison to preserve the San Onofre plant’s rail spur and part of its paved yard through the end of decommissioning, and open talks on a lease for a sand terminal from 2028.
